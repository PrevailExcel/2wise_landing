JAMB synonyms and antonyms: stop losing easy marks

"Nearest in meaning" and "opposite in meaning" questions are some of the most learnable marks in JAMB English. Here's how they work, the traps JAMB sets, and a 5-words-a-day method that sticks.

PEPrevail EjimaduFounder of 2Wise · JAMB tutor at Eilit Hub, Aba · Updated 22 October 2026 · 3 min read

In JAMB's "nearest in meaning" questions, pick the option that could replace the word in that sentence without changing its meaning; in "opposite in meaning" questions, pick the option that reverses it. Watch for the classic trap: a synonym placed among the options of an opposite question. The surest way to improve is to learn about five words a day, with their synonyms, antonyms and an example sentence, and review them over the following weeks.

Use of English carries 100 marks, and vocabulary questions are among the most learnable of them. Unlike comprehension, you can steadily build this skill a few minutes at a time.

How do JAMB synonym and antonym questions work?

JAMB gives you a sentence with one word highlighted, then asks for the option nearest in meaning or opposite in meaning to it. For example:

Choose the option nearest in meaning to the word in italics:
The rain finally abated, so the students hurried home.

A. intensified   B. subsided   C. began   D. flooded

Answer: B. To abate is to become less strong. Note that A, "intensified", is the opposite, placed there to catch anyone who misreads the question.

The traps JAMB loves

  1. The synonym in an opposite question (and the reverse). The most common trap. Underline "nearest" or "opposite" before you look at the options.
  2. The look-alike word. Eminent (famous, respected) vs imminent (about to happen). Discreet (careful, tactful) vs discrete (separate).
  3. The false friend. Infamous doesn't mean "not famous"; it means famous for something bad. Enervate means to weaken, the opposite of what it sounds like.
  4. The context switch. Many words have more than one meaning. JAMB tests the meaning in that sentence.

The 5-words-a-day method

Cramming long word lists the week before JAMB doesn't work: you forget most of them within days. What works is a small daily habit with spaced review:

  1. Learn five words a day. For each: the meaning, two or three synonyms, one or two antonyms, and an example sentence.
  2. Test yourself the same day with JAMB-style questions, including the traps above.
  3. Review each word again after about 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 days. Each review takes seconds, and that's what moves words into long-term memory.

Five words a day for six months is around 900 words, more than enough to make vocabulary questions feel easy.

Frequently asked questions

How do I answer "nearest in meaning" questions in JAMB?

Read the full sentence, decide what the word means in that context, then pick the option that could replace it without changing the sentence's meaning. If two options look close, try each in the sentence.

What is the most common trap in JAMB antonym questions?

Including a synonym among the options. Students recognise a related word and pick it, forgetting the question asked for the opposite. Always re-read whether the question says "nearest" or "opposite".

How many new words should I learn a day for JAMB?

About five. It's small enough to do every day and, over six months, adds up to hundreds of words, especially if you review them on a schedule so you don't forget.

Does context matter in JAMB vocabulary questions?

Yes. Many words have more than one meaning, and JAMB tests the meaning in that particular sentence. Always read the whole sentence before choosing.
