Why students who read for six hours can score lower than students who study for two

Long hours of rereading feel productive, but most of it fades by exam day. Here's the difference between passive and active study, and a two-hour daily routine that actually sticks.

PEPrevail EjimaduFounder of 2Wise · JAMB tutor at Eilit Hub, Aba · Updated 24 October 2026 · 6 min read

Rereading notes builds familiarity, not memory, so it feels like learning but fades within days. Answering questions, explaining topics from memory and correcting your mistakes force your brain to retrieve information, and research shows that makes it last. Two focused hours of recall, timed practice and mistake review will usually beat six hours of reading.

One student spends six hours reading a textbook. Another spends two hours studying, answering questions and correcting mistakes. Which student is more likely to remember what they learnt a week later?

Most people pick the first student. Six hours sounds serious. Parents praise it, and the student goes to bed feeling they have done their duty. But when the test comes, the two-hour student very often wins. Not because they are smarter or care more, but because they spent their time on the kind of work that makes memory stick.

What is the difference between passive and active learning?

Passive learning is when information flows into you: reading, rereading, highlighting, copying notes neatly, watching a lesson video. It isn't useless, because you have to meet information before you can learn it. But on its own it asks very little of your brain.

Active learning is when you have to pull information out of your own head and use it: answering questions, explaining a topic aloud with the book closed, solving problems, working out why you got something wrong. It's harder and feels slower. That difficulty is exactly what builds memory.

What the research found: in a well-known 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students either reread a passage or take recall tests on it. Tested five minutes later, the rereaders did slightly better. Tested two days or a week later, the students who practised recall remembered clearly more. Rereading wins the short game and loses the long one.

Why does rereading your notes feel like learning?

When you read a page for the third time, it feels easy. The words are familiar and you know what's coming next. Your brain reads that ease as proof that you understand.

But recognising something is not the same as remembering it. You can recognise a classmate's face in a crowd without being able to describe it from memory. In the same way, you can recognise every line of your Biology notes and still go blank when a question asks you to explain osmosis in your own words.

In class, I see this often. A student tells me, "Sir, I have read this topic three times." I close the textbook and ask one simple question on it, and the room goes quiet. That student genuinely worked hard. They were practising recognition, and the exam tests recall.

A major 2013 review of common study methods rated rereading and highlighting as low-value, and practice testing and spreading study over time as the two highest-value techniques. Rereading isn't wrong. It's just the weakest way to spend most of your study time.

How do practice questions expose what you don't know?

A question is a torchlight. It shines straight into the dark corners of what you think you know.

When you read about the laws of motion, everything makes sense. When a question asks which law explains why a passenger jerks forward when a bus brakes suddenly, you find out whether it truly does. If you hesitate between two options, you've found a gap. That gap was there all along. Reading hid it; the question revealed it.

At our lesson centre, students learn five new English words every day, each with its meaning, synonyms, antonyms and an example sentence. We don't stop at reading them aloud. The next day, before anything else, students have to use yesterday's words. The ones who only copied them into their notebooks struggle. The ones who tested themselves the night before answer quickly. Same words, same class, different method.

So after you learn a topic, answer questions on it the same day. Getting some wrong isn't failure. It's the most useful information you'll get in the whole session.

Why do your mistakes matter more than your score?

Many students finish a practice test, look at the score, feel happy or sad, and close the book. That's like a doctor taking your temperature and sending you home without treatment. The score tells you how you're doing. The mistakes tell you what to do next.

Picture two students who both score 22 out of 40 in a Chemistry test. The first sighs and moves on to a new test. The second goes through all 18 wrong answers and sorts them:

  • I didn't know it. The topic needs to be learnt again.
  • I knew it but misread the question. Slow down on the question, not the topic.
  • I made a careless error. A sign, a unit or a calculation slipped.
  • I ran out of time. Speed needs work.

The second student finds that 7 of the 18 mistakes came from one topic, mole concept, and 4 came from misreading. On the next test they don't need to be cleverer. They only need to fix two specific problems. That's how scores move from 22 to 30 in a few weeks.

Keep a mistake notebook. For every wrong answer, write the question in short, the correct answer and one sentence on why you missed it. Before any big test, revise that notebook first. It's the most personal study material you'll ever own.

Why should you practise under timed conditions?

Knowing an answer and producing it under pressure are two different skills. Most students only train the first.

At home, with no clock, a student may spend four minutes carefully working out one Physics question and get it right. In JAMB, you have 180 questions in 2 hours, about 40 seconds each. That student hasn't failed to learn Physics. They've failed to practise Physics at exam speed.

When we run our first fully timed mock in class, the results surprise many students. Some who do well in untimed exercises finish with 30 or 40 questions untouched. Others panic at the countdown and change right answers to wrong ones. After four or five timed mocks, both problems shrink. The clock stops being frightening because it's familiar.

You don't need to time every session. But once you've learnt a topic, practise some questions against the clock, and as the exam gets closer, sit full mocks under real conditions: one sitting, no phone, no breaks, no peeping at notes. Our JAMB CBT exam-day tips cover pacing in detail.

What does a good two-hour study routine look like?

This routine combines learning, practice and revision in two focused hours. Put your phone in another room before you start.

  1. Recall warm-up (10 minutes). Without opening any book, write down everything you remember from yesterday's topic. Then check your notes and fill in what you missed. Test yourself on your last five vocabulary words and add five new ones.
  2. Learn one topic actively (40 minutes). Read in short chunks of a page or two. After each chunk, close the book and explain it aloud or write a three-line summary in your own words. If you can't, read that chunk again.
  3. Practise questions (40 minutes). Answer 20 to 30 questions on today's topic, then 10 on older topics so they don't fade. Aim for about 40 seconds per question.
  4. Review your mistakes (20 minutes). Go through every wrong answer and write it into your mistake notebook with the correct answer and why you missed it.
  5. Plan tomorrow (10 minutes). Pick tomorrow's topic, and one weak area from your mistake notebook to revisit.
Once a week: swap the routine for a full timed mock, then spend the next day's session reviewing it.

Notice how little of the two hours is spent simply reading. Most of it goes into recall, questions and corrections. That's why two hours like this can beat six hours with a textbook. For a longer plan built on the same idea, read how to score 300 in JAMB 2027.

Start with one question tonight

The six-hour student isn't wrong to work hard. They're wrong about what hard work looks like. What decides your result isn't hours at the desk. It's how many times you made your brain pull information out, found a gap, and fixed it.

So tonight, before another long reading session, close your notes and ask yourself one question on what you read today. If the answer comes easily, good. If it doesn't, you've just found where your real study should begin.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to read for long hours or study for a shorter time?

What you do with the time matters more than how long you sit. Two focused hours of recall, practice questions and correcting mistakes usually beat many hours of rereading, because recall builds memory and rereading mostly builds familiarity.

What is active recall?

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory without looking, for example by answering questions or explaining a topic aloud with your book closed. Research on the testing effect shows it improves long-term memory more than rereading.

How many hours should I study for JAMB every day?

Two focused hours a day, every day, is enough for steady progress if most of that time goes into practice questions and reviewing mistakes. Add one full timed mock each week as the exam gets closer.

Should I stop rereading my notes completely?

No. You need to read a topic before you can practise it. Just keep reading short, then close the book and test yourself. Rereading on its own feels productive but fades quickly.
